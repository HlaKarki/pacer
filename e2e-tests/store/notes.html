Notes on paced reading

Pacing is the simplest reading aid there is. Something moves along the line at a steady speed, and the eyes follow it. For most of history that something was a finger, and it still works: the finger gives the eye a place to land and makes drifting back take effort.

In the late 1950s, Evelyn Wood turned the finger into a method. She taught readers to sweep a hand down the page and keep their eyes on it, and her courses made speed reading a household phrase.

Screens brought a second approach. Rapid serial visual presentation, or RSVP, flashes one word at a time in a fixed spot, so the eyes never move at all. It is easy to set up and easy to measure, which made it popular in research and later in apps.

RSVP has a cost. With no line to look back along, a missed word is gone, and the sentence often goes with it. Readers can't see how long a paragraph runs, and they can't slow down on a hard passage without stopping the stream.

A highlighter pacer keeps the page. It marks the words at the pace you set while the rest of the text stays in place, so a glance back costs a fraction of a second. You get the steady push of a finger without the finger.

Good pacing also bends to the text. Short common words like "the" and "of" need less time than long or rare ones, and the end of a sentence is a natural place to pause. Spreading the time this way keeps the average where you set it, while the hard words get the time they need.

Pick a pace a little above your own speed and let the highlighter pull you along. Raise it when the text is easy, lower it when the text is dense, and stop whenever you want to think about what you just read.
