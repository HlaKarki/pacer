How we read

Your eyes jump when you read

Reading feels like a smooth glide along the line. It is really a string of quick hops and short stops, and that changes how you can speed it up.

7 min read

Watch someone read and their eyes seem to slide across the page. Film them up close and the slide breaks apart. The eyes hop forward a few letters at a time, stop, take in what's there, and hop again. The hops are called saccades, and the stops are called fixations.

A saccade is fast. It lasts about 30 milliseconds, and you are close to blind while it happens. Everything you take from a page arrives during fixations, which last about a quarter of a second each. A practiced reader makes three or four of them every second.

Each fixation sees less than it feels like. Sharp vision covers only a few letters around the point you look at. In English, you can use about 15 letters to the right of that point and about 4 to the left. Past that, words are blurred shapes, good enough to plan the next jump and not much more.

The slips back

About one jump in seven goes backward. Some of these regressions are useful: a word didn't fit, or a sentence turned in a way you didn't expect. Many are just drift. The eye loses its place, slips back to a word it already read, and the line takes longer than it needed to.

This is why the oldest speed reading trick still works. Run a finger under the line as you read, and the eye follows it. The finger sets a steady pace, gives the eye a place to land, and makes drifting back take effort.

A pacer does the same job without the finger. It moves through the text at a set speed, and your eyes follow it. The words around it stay where they are, so a glance back is still there when you need one. It just stops being a habit.

How fast is fast

Speed has a limit. Past about 600 words a minute, understanding usually drops off and reading turns into skimming. The useful gain is smaller and steadier: fewer wasted jumps, fewer slips backward, and a pace you choose instead of one your attention wanders into.

Most people read prose at 200 to 300 words a minute. A pacer set a little above your own speed pulls you along without leaving you behind. Raise it when the text is easy, lower it when it is dense, and let the hard words take the time they need.
